By the Editors of Classic Chicago Magazine · Presented by @properties

On the northeast corner of Astor and Goethe, a block from the lake, stands one of the Gold Coast’s most private addresses: an eleven-residence Art Deco cooperative that Philip B. Maher designed in 1928 and completed in 1929. High inside it is an apartment that has had only three owners in nearly a century. On October 26, 2026, @properties listed it as coming soon. Its next owner will be the fourth.

David Adler designed the residence for Potter Palmer II and his wife, Pauline, and by 1930 it was ready for them. It spreads across two principal levels and roughly 7,000 square feet, with eight bedrooms, six full baths and a powder room. To walk through it is to walk through a century of Chicago: the Palmers who built State Street and the Gold Coast, the Mortons who built an empire on salt, and even a president’s rooms from the Palmer House.

“A home that has had only three owners in nearly a century. Its next owner will be the fourth.”

Arriving

The apartment announces itself before you are inside it. The elevator opens onto an entry of mirror and steel that Adler designed for the Palmers, a small, glittering room in the Art Deco spirit of the building around it. Beyond it the scale changes completely. The foyer rises nineteen feet, and a curved staircase, now historically designated, sweeps up from the floor to the level above. It is the gesture of a great house, not an apartment, and it sets the tone for everything that follows.

The Main Level

The living room is large enough for two fireplaces. It is framed by Aegean marble columns brought from the Palmer estate, and it holds room for formal dining. Off it is a richly paneled library with a fireplace of its own and a powder room, as suited to an office as to an evening by the fire.

The service rooms are as generous as the public ones. The kitchen has storage on a level above it, there is a large laundry room, and two en-suite bedrooms complete the floor, for guests or for a household with staff.

Upstairs

The upper level is given over to private rooms. The primary suite has its own fireplace, a walk-in closet, dressing rooms and its own bath. There are three further bedrooms, two with their own baths and one that would serve equally well as a study. Further staircases link the levels, in the manner of a townhouse rather than a flat.

A Museum of Details

What sets the home apart is how much of the past it has kept. There are original African ebony and French château wood floors, intricately carved woodwork, ornate fireplaces, Art Deco bathrooms and lighting, Delft tiles, and marble and granite throughout. In the grand salon hangs nineteenth-century Chinese Chippendale wallpaper, installed by the second owner, Suzette Morton Davidson. And built into the rooms are a door, a fireplace, paneling and millwork from Ulysses S. Grant’s suite at the Palmer House. Chicago magazine has counted the residence among the five finest houses in the city.

The Palmers and the Gold Coast

No family shaped this part of the city more than the Palmers. Potter Palmer (1826–1902) opened a dry-goods store in Chicago in 1852, went into partnership with Marshall Field and Levi Leiter in 1865, and then set about remaking downtown. He moved the city’s main commercial district from Lake Street to State Street, which he widened and lined with his own buildings for three-quarters of a mile. In 1871 he married Bertha Honôré, and that October the Great Fire destroyed nearly everything he had built. He borrowed $1.7 million, at the time the largest sum ever lent to a private individual, and rebuilt, the Palmer House first among them.

Palmer then turned north, to the marshy land along the lake. Lake Shore Drive opened in 1875, and in 1885 the Palmers moved into a castle of their own design there. Chicago society followed. Prairie Avenue gave way as the city’s most fashionable address, and within two decades Astor Street, on land Palmer had helped develop, was lined with mansions. The Gold Coast, in a real sense, began with the Palmers.

Bertha Palmer became one of the great collectors of her age, an early champion of the French Impressionists. Their son Potter Palmer II, born in 1875, carried on both sides of the family legacy. He was a director of the First National Bank of Chicago and president of the Art Institute, and in 1922 he and his brother, Honôré, gave the museum thirty-two paintings from their parents’ collection. He married Pauline Kohlsaat.

In 1928 Potter Palmer II sold the Lake Shore Drive castle, keeping the right to lease it back, and that same year Maher was drawing 1301 North Astor. By 1930 the Palmers’ new rooms there were ready. Potter Palmer II lived until 1943.

A President’s Rooms

The pieces of the Palmer House in the apartment are a family story. In 1874 Ulysses S. Grant’s son Frederick married Bertha Palmer’s sister, Ida Honôré. Five years later, on November 13, 1879, Potter and Bertha Palmer welcomed Grant home from his journey around the world with what has been called the greatest banquet in American history: some five hundred guests at the Palmer House, with Generals Sherman and Sheridan at the tables and Mark Twain among the speakers. Half a century later the Palmers’ son carried a door, a fireplace and the paneling of the president’s suite into his own home, a very Chicago kind of heirloom.

Maher’s Corner

Philip Brooks Maher (1894–1981) was born in Kenilworth, the son of George W. Maher, one of the leading figures of the Prairie School. He studied architecture at the University of Michigan, served in the army, traveled in Europe and in 1921 became his father’s partner. Where the elder Maher had worked in the low, horizontal language of the Prairie, the son turned upward and toward Art Deco. He was made a Fellow of the American Institute of Architects in 1940.

1301 North Astor is the purest expression of that turn: fifteen stories of pale limestone, its narrow piers rising unbroken to the roofline, and just eleven residences inside. In 1931 Maher completed a second cooperative diagonally across the intersection, at 1260 North Astor. Between them they made the corner of Astor and Goethe his; these pages once called it Philip Maher’s Astor Street corner.

The cooperative form suited the street. Each household owns shares in the building rather than a deed to a unit, the residents approve their new neighbors, and the building stays small, quiet and private, as it has since 1929.

David Adler

Original buyers were invited to shape their own apartments, and the Palmers turned to David Adler (1882–1949), the most sought-after residential architect in the Chicago region. He had trained at Princeton from 1900 to 1904, in Munich, and at the École des Beaux-Arts in Paris from 1906 to 1911. Over thirty-five years he designed more than two hundred buildings, among them the great country houses of Lake Forest and Lake Bluff and, in 1928, Castle Hill in Ipswich, Massachusetts. From 1925 he was a trustee of the Art Institute, the museum Potter Palmer II also served as president. He was made a Fellow of the American Institute of Architects in 1941.

Adler worked elsewhere in the building too. On the second floor he designed an apartment for the painter Frederic Clay Bartlett, the collector who in 1926 gave the Art Institute Georges Seurat’s A Sunday on La Grande Jatte. Bartlett painted a mural for its dining room, and the film critic Gene Siskel, who bought that apartment in the early 1980s, found it still there.

The Mortons

The apartment’s second owner, at mid-century, brought a second great Chicago fortune to its rooms. Suzette Morton Davidson, known to her friends as Susie, was the daughter of Sterling Morton (1885–1961) and Preston Owsley Morton, and the granddaughter of Joy Morton (1855–1934), who incorporated the Morton Salt Company in 1910 and founded the Morton Arboretum in Lisle in 1922. Her great-grandfather was J. Sterling Morton, who helped establish Arbor Day and served as Secretary of Agriculture under Grover Cleveland from 1893 to 1897.

Susie Morton was a woman of real taste and purpose. A Vassar graduate, she spent four years studying typography and design at the School of the Art Institute. She joined the Arboretum’s board in 1939 and, after her father’s death in 1961, chaired it until 1977. In 1962 the family’s gift of the Morton Wing opened at the Art Institute, and in 2000 the Arboretum named its special collections in her honor. At 1301 North Astor it was she who hung the Chinese Chippendale wallpaper that still lines the grand salon.

Astor Street

Astor Street takes its name from John Jacob Astor, the fur and real estate magnate who never lived in Chicago. It runs only a few quiet blocks, from Division Street to North Avenue, yet it holds one of the finest concentrations of residential architecture in the country: Queen Anne, Richardsonian Romanesque and Georgian Revival mansions from the decades after Potter Palmer drew society north, and, from the 1920s, the elegant Art Deco co-ops that replaced some of them. The city designated the Astor Street District a Chicago Landmark on December 19, 1975.

Bob Glaze walked these blocks for us in “Historic Residential Architecture in Chicago’s Neighborhoods” on October 18, 2026, and nearly every stop on his route is a few steps from 1301. Directly across Astor stand the James L. Houghteling Houses at 1308–12, three townhouses that Burnham & Root designed in 1887 and 1888. Up the block, at 1365, is the James Charnley House, designed by Louis Sullivan with his young draftsman Frank Lloyd Wright in 1891 and 1892, a National Historic Landmark and now the headquarters of the Society of Architectural Historians. At 1406 is the French-style house that David Adler completed in 1921 for Joseph T. Ryerson Jr., and at 1444 the Russell House, an Art Deco design by Holabird & Root of 1928 and 1929, exactly contemporary with 1301. At the north end, at 1500, the Patterson-McCormick Mansion of 1891 by Stanford White was later bought by Cyrus H. McCormick.

Behind the mansions at the top of the street is one of Chicago’s oddest treasures, the Wooden Alley. Laid in 1909 in cedar blocks, at a cost of $3,346.96, it runs between Astor and State, behind the residence of the Archbishop of Chicago. Wood-block paving was common in the city from the 1850s; this is one of the very few stretches left. It was listed on the National Register of Historic Places on May 22, 2002 and restored in 2011, and the original blocks survive on its west side.

Across the Street: Goudy Square

From the windows of 1301 one looks across Goethe Street to Goudy Square Park, on the southeast corner of Astor and Goethe. It is one of the oldest public spaces on the North Side. The developer H. O. Stone dedicated the land to the city as parkland in 1847, when it was known as Union Square and later as Astor Square. By the time the Chicago Park District took it over in 1934 it was called Goudy Park, after William C. Goudy (1824–1893), the attorney who served as president of the Lincoln Park Board from 1887 to 1893. Today its 0.61 acres hold a playground the Park District designed in 1991 and renewed in 2014, a green front lawn for one of the city’s most formal corners.

The lakefront is a block east, and Oak Street Beach and the Magnificent Mile are a short walk south.
