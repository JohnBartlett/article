Short Story

Edgelord

By Scott Holleran  •  September 6, 2026

Tommy sat at the center of what had become his world—a gaming console. As he did, fingers and thumbs moving in rapid succession, Tommy felt a thrill. With each character image popping in his direct or peripheral vision, he fired, halted or paused, dodging, darting and aiming again and again, activating his brain, nerves and digits all at once. Taking occasional gulps of soda as his hunched, sedentary body repetitively moved, pinching his neck, hands and joints, Tommy’s rapid response stimulated his adrenaline. The rush and fury came in waves as he downed characters, one by one, glancing at his kill count, immersed in badges, noises, colors, flashes, droops and chat bubbles encompassing his dark, devoid world.

Tommy was in heaven. Or was it hell? For Tommy, truth was hard to tell. He didn’t know or care anymore. He hadn’t since before his parents divorced, leaving him and two brothers fractured, disengaged and lost in various spaces in Michigan, Colorado, Tennessee, Massachusetts and Idaho. Nothing mattered to Tommy, who sat on a sofa in an apartment his dad paid for where the walls were as blank as Tommy’s mind.

Bindi used to come over and mess around in play they’d discovered by accident upon gaming one day after class. They had attended one of those mandatory college classroom drills before the fuss of overhyped technology made corruption all but customary on tests for his engineering degree, which was almost ready to bestow. Bindi lost interest and had stopped coming over. Tommy would graduate in another empty gesture next spring. Right now, Tommy could not have known that the sky was gray with massive clouds slowly moving east in the mid-morning of another day over the state school’s off-campus housing. Rain had stopped late last night as he stayed up gaming. Tommy ignored the world beyond his blackout curtains. A table between his kitchen and the sofa had been his food delivery hub—a sole connection to reality. Tommy had noticed Bindi noticing him during orientation last year. The pre-med student was petite, which he liked.

Tommy was not. His large frame could’ve been perfectly proportioned had he followed a food and fitness regimen. Tommy, like his mother, ate excessive amounts of food and, like his brother Greg, drank beer, soda and energy drinks, so he was anxious, jacked and sleep-deprived. Gaming exacerbated Tommy’s condition. When he did exercise, he lifted weights strictly for superficial reasons. In the harsh light of a bathroom, he saw that his skin flopped over the elastic of his pants. Bindi had liked the wavy curls in his brown hair and his muscles when she used to come over. Now, he was merely big with muscles reduced to soft folds of flabby flesh.

“You ok?” His younger brother Cole messaged. Tommy ignored the message and kept pressing rubber dots on the gaming console. He heard chatter in the outside hall and the sound of laughter from the clearing in front of his apartment building. Cole had placed a question mark over the message. This irritated Tommy because it caused him to pause. Annoyance turned to frustration. By his reckoning, Tommy knew that he and his younger brother were being forced to move yet again after graduation to live with his dad in Missouri. Tommy glanced at the rifle leaning against the wall by the front door. His dad had bought it a year ago during a road trip, another of his dad’s ideas. Tommy was planning to use the rifle to kill everyone in the cafeteria before shooting himself so he didn’t have to move. He’d made plans and procured ammunition. He’d started writing a manifesto, though Tommy lacked motivation to finish.

For now, Tommy could hear pops, three in a row. Then, another round, which caught his attention and made Tommy pause, which made him feel more alert than annoyed. He thought of Cole’s message and, for the first time, he looked toward the blackout curtains, which covered a window facing north overlooking the campus across the avenue. A few more pops sounded, followed by a siren and another series of pops. Shots were getting closer. Tommy could hear chatter in the hall. He realized that the laughter he thought he’d heard from the clearing below the building had been screaming. That’s when he heard, in rapid succession, a series of slamming doors and thumping like a herd of runners coming through the hall and that’s when Tommy heard pounding on his front door. Tommy went to the door. He looked in the peephole. He ignored what he saw and turned from the door.

Suddenly, what Tommy heard caught his notice and this changed the course of Tommy’s life. “Help,” a soft voice pleaded. “Please let me in.” The voice, sounding at once prayerful and insistent, added: “I want to live.” Tommy opened the door because he wanted to know why. Reaching over a small person’s shoulder, Tommy scooped the slim, petite body toward him and shut the door. Turning to the sweating, shivering young man in his presence, he asked: “Why?”

The lean young man, breathing heavily and shaking, looking puzzled, then, perturbed—muttering, “are you serious?”—became curious. He replied: “Because I want to dance.”

This is when Tommy noticed his terrified guest noticing the rifle by the door. “You dance,” he said to the guest, who nodded, side-glancing the gun, then, the door and, still in a stupor, looked around the dark apartment. “May I sit?” Tommy nodded. The man, perspiring in an off-white, one-piece leotard, collapsed on Tommy’s spot on the sofa and shook. Tommy pointed to a fleece blanket on the sofa’s arm. The man reached for it, enshrouding himself, and said, “bodies—blood—everywhere.” Tommy took a step from the door toward the sofa. He listened. “I was in rehearsal,” the man said. Tommy observed that the guest was curling into a fetal position, shaking, going into shock, which Tommy knew because Tommy had knowledge of this condition.

“Hold up,” Tommy said, extending his arm and putting his hand out as a gesture to stop. Tommy turned, dead-bolting and chaining the door. “You need to come down,” he told the man, who nodded and exhaled, saying, “thank you for letting me inside your home. I’m sorry for the trouble. Thank you.” Tommy wanted the man to stop shaking and stop thanking him. Tommy came around to the front of the sofa, which caused the small man to look up to him, which seemed to soothe him. “Thank you,” he said to Tommy between breaths. “I’m—I’m scared.” This was the first time since his mother apologized for screaming at him during a custody hearing that someone expressed an emotion to Tommy. Tommy sat down beside the man, which seemed to calm him. “May I have a glass of water?” He asked.

This prompted Tommy to think. He felt a swell in his chest. He paused to think about the concept glass. Then, he chose to think about the concept water. “Yes,” he said, as if to himself, “I have those.” As if by instinct, Tommy reached for the gaming remote control and pressed the power off, slowly, steadily rising, turning and entering his kitchen until he stood before a cabinet. He opened a cabinet door and retrieved a glass, turning toward the refrigerator, placing the glass under a nozzle and pressing the glass against a piece of plastic. Water filled the glass.

“Here,” he said, as the man took the glass in both hands, causing the blanket to fall from his shoulders, trembling as he held the glass, spilling water on the sofa. Tommy scowled, taking the glass from the man. “No, here.” Tommy took a seat beside him, bringing the glass of water to his lips. “I’m sorry, I’m scared,” the man said. To put the guest at ease, Tommy gently placed his other hand on the top of the man’s spine, guiding him toward the edge of the glass, “drink slowly,” he said, holding and tilting the glass as the man drank water and then smiled, his eyes filling with tears. A thud sounded above, causing them both to jolt. Tommy heard pops in the distance. The upstairs thumping had ceased. Rapid gunfire had also stopped. The men paused as Tommy slowly nodded, setting the glass on a coaster. “You’re safe here,” he told the man. “Just breathe.”

The man slowly inhaled, tears spilling over as he looked into Tommy’s eyes and exhaled through his mouth, filling the space between them with the scent of spearmint. The man slowly nodded as tears traced his cheeks and Tommy noticed that his light brown eyes were trusting and wide. Suddenly, Tommy became aware that the swelling in his chest became a grumbling in his stomach.

“What’s your name?” He asked. “Galdu,” the man answered. “Tom here,” Tommy said. “I’ve got sushi from last night and some chips.” “I’m not hungry,” Galdu said. Tommy persisted. “You need to eat. You’re all nerves. Where’s your phone?” Galdu shook his head. “In my bag at Hadley Hall. Wh—where am I?” Tommy took him by the shoulders. “You’re off campus at Canaan Place apartments across from campus,” he said sternly, “that’s a couple of miles from Hadley. Listen, I’m going to feed you, then I’m going to give you some medication and you will need to lie down.” Galdu sniffled as he nodded. “OK, Tom.”

No one had called him Tom since junior year of high school. That was the time he was on both the debate team and the cheerleading squad. Tommy was a muscular, agile cheerleader, lifting pretty girls during halftime and scoring points against nerds arguing for socialism. Varsity football players were jealous of both Tommy’s intelligence and his strong, A-framed physique. “Nothing to it,” Tommy replied, standing up.

Galdu ate everything Tommy prepared. He took the sedatives. He laid down on the sofa after Tommy arranged a pillow, blankets and sheets. Cocoa-skinned Galdu even fell asleep while Tommy read news of the siege, messaging his brother Cole, his dad and his mom that he was fine. He did not message that he was also host to a fellow student, let alone that he was curious about mass shooting or that he had harbored the same purpose. Hearing a couple of distant pops while reading that the shooter or shooters remained at large, Tommy glanced at the rifle by the door as he listened to Galdu’s gentle breathing while his guest slept. Pausing to reflect, Tommy put himself deep in thought. Tommy devised and revised his plan.

Galdu awakened an hour later. Rumors had circulated that another massacre was underway on the other side of campus with the shooter or band of shooters remaining at large. Police and tactical teams had been dispatched on patrol in pursuit of the perpetrator or perpetrators. The area was on lockdown. Sirens wailed as Galdu rose and stood to stretch. “May I look outside?” He asked Tommy, still seated at the table immersed in thought. Tommy nodded. Galdu stood before the blackout curtain and reached over, pulling the curtain to the side. “Nothing’s moving,” he said, looking down at campus. “Thank you for everything,” he said, turning and smiling awkwardly. “Is it OK if I play some music?” Tommy nodded, looking down to scroll on his device. “Talk to the speaker,” he told Galdu.

Galdu gave a voice command. The speaker played some rhythm and blues. Galdu spun into a dance, his leg rising as one foot pointed down and the other up and outward while his pirouettes turned to the musical loops in the room. He glided across the floor as Tommy watched in shock, wonder and mild alarm. “What are you doing?” He asked his guest. “Dancing for life,” came Galdu’s breathless, joyful reply. “I can see that,” Tommy heard himself saying. Galdu danced for 13 minutes as songs changed and the dancer danced throughout the apartment, tumbling to the floor with a knee bent and leg extended, arching his back and rising as his arms extended into the air. At one point, he leaped over the sofa, landing with precision in front of the gaming console on the coffee table. Galdu turned to Tommy, beaming with a bright smile and a laugh before taking a bow. Tommy felt compelled to stop and render a round of applause. “Bravo,” he said, surprised at the tone of gaiety in his voice. “That was beautiful.”

Galdu gave a single, dismissive wave of his left hand, shrugged and stopped the music as he caught his breath. Then, he walked to the window. Spreading his arms wide, Galdu opened the blackout curtains. For the first time since he lived there, Tommy saw his home in daylight, the sun’s rays bolting between puffy white clouds onto every surface in the apartment, pouring color, light and brightness over the rooms. Giving a new command, glorious, sweeping music tinkled throughout his home and Tommy sat back, the rifle on the edge of his peripheral vision, as he looked at the smiling dancer in the center of the floor, curving and raising an arm with a hand cupping upward in a gesture to the sky and the light and the sun, as if in salutation to—and exaltation of—the whole of the world.

Suddenly, they heard the sound of a man’s shriek. A rapid series of shots sounded in the exterior hallway, followed by screams, thuds and moaning. Tommy reached for his rifle as he caught the look of horror on Galdu’s face and he whispered low with severity: “Down!” to the dancer, gesturing to the floor as gunfire hammered against the door, which was then kicked open. A man in a police uniform stood with feet apart spraying gunfire across Tommy’s home.

Pausing the assault, stepping inside with bloodied boots amid the gunsmoke, Tommy’s bloodied body lay motionless on top of Galdu, whose leotard bled red. As the murderer stepped over their entangled bodies—Tommy had shielded Galdu’s with his own—the killer was struck by sunlight over the campus and, for a second, the shooter paused at the sound of a concerto in deliverance. At this moment, Galdu crawled, heaved and lurched, grabbing the rifle from Tom’s hands and aiming where he knew the shooter would fall down and, with a single gunshot, firing and felling the killer. Galdu in his bloodied leotard rose, pointing the gun down and, closing his eyes, firing at the center of the killer’s forehead.

“Up!” He called to his savior Tom, lying still on the floor. “Up! Tom, get up!!” Galdu could see the struggle to breathe, so he retrieved Tom’s phone and called the emergency line before couching down to Tom’s side. “I’m alive,” the gamer weakly said. “I know,” Galdu said, smiling in reply, looking at the door as he answered: “Canaan Place housing, apartment 303.” “I want to live,” Tommy added. Galdu gripped Tom’s hand, which he raised and then kissed. “You will…and you do—” Galdu sweetly told him, “I know you do.”
