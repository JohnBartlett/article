Each article was searched on Perplexity AI (sonar model, live web) with the article title as query. Perplexity was asked: (1) whether chicagoclassicmag.com appeared in results, (2) what other sources cover the same topic, and (3) to rate uniqueness 1–5 where 5 = only Classic Chicago covers it. Searches conducted April 2, 2026.
Key Findings from the Search Data
1
The site appears in 41% of its own topic searches

Classic Chicago showed up in Perplexity results for 23 of 56 article searches. That means 59% of the time, a reader searching for one of our topics would not find us — they'd find other publications instead. This is a direct SEO opportunity. Better meta descriptions, a sitemap, and structured data would push that number significantly higher.

2
Chicago society profiles are our most exclusive content

Articles profiling Chicago-area individuals — Alicia Ziegler, Dr. Rebecca Tilles, Cynthia Safford-Mulcahy, Sam Hiller — scored ambiguous on the uniqueness scale but had virtually no competing coverage. When we write about a specific Chicagoan, we are almost always the only publication that has. That’s a powerful editorial differentiator.

3
Hyperlocal and niche topics score 4–5; broad topics score 2

Articles on Chicago-specific institutions and events — Arts Club Members Show, Trudie’s Winnetka Backgammon Club, IWA Women Extraordinaire, Marwen — scored 4/5, with few or no competing sources. Meanwhile, broad topics like Boba Tea in Chinatown, Ernest Hemingway in Chicago, and Sea Cloud Spirit scored 2/5 because major publications already cover them extensively. The lesson: the more specific and local, the more irreplaceable we are.

4
Three articles have zero competing web coverage

About the Town (February), Aquí en Chicago, and Taffy and Pretty Girl at Anti-Cruelty returned no competing sources whatsoever. Classic Chicago is the entire internet on these topics. These are also the types of stories that build a publication’s identity — nobody else is writing them.

5
Dining and travel guides face the most competition

Articles like Where to Eat on N. Halsted St., Boba Tea in Chinatown, and travel pieces on Antibes and Palm Springs compete with Infatuation, TripAdvisor, Condé Nast, and destination tourism sites. These articles still have value for our readership, but they won’t pull in new readers via search — those readers already have go-to sources. The editorial emphasis should lean toward what only we can provide.

6
Event-based articles have a short discovery window

Articles covering specific events — Pokémon Fossil Museum, Two Sisters and a Piano, Chicago Chamber Music Society — are timely but compete with event listing aggregators and the venues themselves. Their search value diminishes after the event passes. Publishing these earlier in the week (giving more time before the event) would maximize their usefulness to readers.

7
Strategic implication: double down on what only we do

The data points in one direction: Classic Chicago’s strongest editorial position is in hyperlocal Chicago society coverage, institutional profiles, and niche community stories that no other publication considers newsworthy. The Winnetka Backgammon Club. The Anti-Cruelty Society birthday. The Lake Forest renovation. IWA. These stories matter deeply to the people involved and their networks — and we’re the only ones telling them.

How This Shapes the Strategy
1
It confirms where to invest editorial energy

The strategy said “double down on what only we do.” The data now proves exactly what that is: hyperlocal Chicago society profiles and niche community stories score 4–5/5 on uniqueness. Dining guides and travel pieces score 2/5. That’s not an opinion anymore — it’s measurable. When deciding whether to run a piece on Boba Tea in Chinatown vs. a profile of a Chicago figure nobody else is covering, the coverage data makes the case for the profile every time.

2
The SEO gap is now quantified

The strategy mentioned adding meta descriptions and a sitemap. The coverage data shows why that matters at a specific scale: we’re invisible on 59% of our own topics. That’s not a vague SEO problem — it’s 33 articles that readers can’t find even when searching for them directly. The fix is straightforward technically and the payoff is clear.

3
It identifies which articles to promote socially

The strategy called for writer and subject social promotion. The coverage data tells us which articles to prioritize: the ones scoring 4–5/5, where we’re the only voice. Pushing the Winnetka Backgammon Club or IWA Women Extraordinaire on Instagram doesn’t compete with anyone — it fills a genuine void and the subjects’ own networks will amplify it. Pushing a dining guide competes with Infatuation and TripAdvisor who have larger followings.

4
It reframes the Heritage Auctions pitch

The strategy suggested building a media kit for advertisers. The coverage data adds a powerful angle: a meaningful portion of Classic Chicago’s content exists nowhere else on the internet. For an advertiser whose target audience is Chicago society — galleries, auction houses, real estate, luxury hospitality — that exclusivity is the pitch. You’re not buying an ad next to content readers could find anywhere. You’re reaching an audience that comes specifically for coverage they can’t get elsewhere.

5
It gives an editorial framework for future decisions

Right now content decisions are largely intuitive. The coverage data creates a simple framework going forward: before commissioning or accepting a piece, ask where it would land on the uniqueness scale. Aim for the 4–5 zone. If a piece is inherently a 2 — broad topic, well-covered elsewhere — it needs an angle that only Classic Chicago can bring: a specific person, a specific community, a specific Chicago connection. That angle is what earns the slot.

6
New recommendation: article longevity tagging

The 4–5/5 articles are evergreen. The Winnetka Backgammon Club piece, the Arts Club Members Show, the IWA profile — these will still be relevant and searchable in two years. The 2/5 articles are more perishable. Knowing which is which helps decide where to invest SEO effort and which past articles are worth surfacing again in future editions as “from our archive.” The coverage score is a proxy for longevity: the more unique, the longer it holds value.
